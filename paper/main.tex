\begin{abstract}
Adult spinal deformity (ASD) alters gait, yet video-based screening models generally rely on appearance cues and provide limited correspondence with clinically meaningful body regions. We propose PoseGated, a clinically guided video classification framework that integrates RGB gait videos with clinician-defined region-of-interest priors constructed from body keypoints. PoseGated uses learnable channel-wise gates to selectively inject these priors into the visual backbone and auxiliary joint-specific side heads to align intermediate attention with clinician annotations.
We evaluate the method on a three-class clinical gait dataset using patient-grouped three-fold cross-validation. PoseGated achieves $94.8\%$ clip-level accuracy, improving over the RGB-only backbone ($90.7\%$) by $4.1$ percentage points and outperforming the evaluated attention-fusion baselines. Extensive ablations reveal that the effectiveness of clinical priors depends strongly on their injection depth: fusion restricted to the stem and first residual stage performs best, whereas fusion at every stage yields the weakest PoseGated configuration. Additional background and temporal-smoothness objectives and strongly RGB-biased gate initialisation also reduce classification accuracy.
On held-out patients, the supervised attention maps accurately recover the clinician-defined regions, with a pointing-game hit rate of $1.00$, a pixel-wise ROC AUC of approximately $0.998$, and an attention-map correlation of up to $0.76$. These findings show that selective shallow fusion can translate sparse clinical knowledge into both improved gait classification and anatomically grounded model explanations, supporting interpretable video-based assessment of ASD.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Adult spinal deformity (ASD) is associated with abnormal posture and gait, and its early identification can support timely orthopedic assessment and management~\citep{asd_clinical_review}. Full-spine radiography is central to the structural evaluation of ASD, while marker-based motion capture enables detailed quantitative gait analysis. However, both require specialised equipment and clinical workflows, limiting their applicability to frequent, large-scale, or opportunistic screening~\citep{motioncapture_gait}. In contrast, monocular walking videos can be acquired inexpensively using widely available cameras, making them a promising basis for accessible gait-based assessment. This has motivated the development of video-based gait recognition and classification methods~\citep{clinical_gait_review,chao2019gaitset}, supported by advances in spatiotemporal representation learning~\citep{feichtenhofer2019slowfast}.

Despite their predictive potential, existing video-based models are predominantly appearance driven and provide limited evidence that their decisions are based on clinically meaningful anatomical patterns. Such models may exploit visual cues that correlate with the target labels without necessarily attending to the body regions used by clinicians during gait assessment. Generic channel- or self-attention mechanisms~\citep{hu2018senet,vaswani2017attention} can improve feature selection, but their learned attention is not explicitly constrained to correspond to clinical anatomy. Consequently, high classification performance alone does not establish that a model has learned clinically relevant evidence, which limits its interpretability and potential clinical trustworthiness.

To address this limitation, we investigate whether clinician-defined anatomical priors can be incorporated directly into a spatiotemporal gait classifier. Specifically, orthopedic experts identify clinically relevant regions associated with the lumbar, pelvic, head, and shoulder areas, which are represented as region-of-interest (ROI) attention maps derived from body keypoints. We propose PoseGated, a clinically guided fusion framework that combines RGB video features with these keypoint-derived attention priors through learnable channel-wise gates. The gates allow the network to adaptively control the contribution of visual appearance and clinical prior information at different feature channels. In addition, auxiliary side heads predict joint-specific attention maps at intermediate network stages and are directly supervised using the clinician-defined ROI annotations. This design makes the learned spatial attention quantitatively verifiable rather than relying solely on post-hoc visualisation.

A central question in clinically guided representation learning is not only whether prior knowledge should be incorporated, but also where and how strongly it should influence the network. Clinical priors introduced too early may constrain low-level visual representations, whereas priors introduced too deeply may have limited influence on the learned spatiotemporal features. Similarly, additional regularisation objectives or strongly biased fusion gates may appear beneficial but can overconstrain optimisation or suppress complementary RGB information. We therefore systematically examine the location and depth of prior injection, gate initialisation, and auxiliary supervision strategies. The resulting analysis challenges the intuitive assumption that more extensive prior integration necessarily produces better performance: selective shallow fusion at the stem and first residual stage performs best, whereas fusion across all stages, additional background and temporal-smoothness losses, and strongly RGB-biased gate initialisation reduce classification accuracy.

Beyond classification performance, we evaluate whether the learned attention generalises to unseen patients and remains consistent with the clinician-defined anatomical regions. Using held-out interpretability analysis, we quantify spatial agreement through complementary localisation and similarity measures, including the pointing-game hit rate, pixel-wise area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, and attention-map correlation. This evaluation provides direct evidence of whether the incorporated clinical prior is retained within the learned representation and expressed in the model's intermediate attention maps.

The main contributions of this work are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item We introduce PoseGated, a clinically guided spatiotemporal classification framework that adaptively fuses RGB gait features with keypoint-derived anatomical priors using channel-wise gating.
\item We develop joint-specific auxiliary side heads that align intermediate model attention with clinician-defined ROI annotations, enabling the learned clinical attention to be quantitatively evaluated.
\item We conduct a systematic investigation of prior-injection location and depth, gate initialisation, and auxiliary loss design, showing that selective shallow fusion with direct ROI supervision is more effective than extensive multi-stage fusion and additional regularisation.
\item We perform a held-out interpretability evaluation demonstrating that the supervised attention localises clinically relevant anatomical regions in previously unseen patients, providing anatomically grounded evidence for the model's predictions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Video action and gait recognition}
Modelling motion from video has been driven by spatiotemporal
convolutional networks, from early {3D} CNNs~\citep{tran2015c3d} and inflated
{2D} architectures~\citep{carreira2017i3d}, through factorised
$(2\!+\!1)$D convolutions~\citep{tran2018closer} and very deep {3D}
ResNets~\citep{hara2018can} that transfer the {ImageNet}-scale pretraining
recipe~\citep{deng2009imagenet} to video, to the two-pathway {SlowFast}
family~\citep{feichtenhofer2019slowfast} that our backbone belongs to. Two-stream
designs that separate appearance and motion~\citep{simonyan2014twostream} and
non-local operators that aggregate long-range spatiotemporal
context~\citep{wang2018nonlocal} are widely used, and video
transformers~\citep{arnab2021vivit,bertasius2021timesformer} have more recently
matched or exceeded {3D} CNNs on large corpora such as
{Kinetics}~\citep{kay2017kinetics}. These backbones underpin gait-based
recognition, e.g.\ set-based cross-view gait
identification~\citep{chao2019gaitset}, and our own prior gait
work~\citep{chen2023two,chen2024phasemix}. We adopt a {Kinetics}-pretrained
{SlowR50} as a strong, standard backbone and focus on \emph{how a clinical prior
should be fused into it} rather than on the backbone itself.

\subsection{Clinical and pathological gait analysis}
Clinical gait assessment has traditionally relied on marker-based optical motion
capture and full-spine radiography~\citep{motioncapture_gait}, which are accurate
but costly and ill-suited to opportunistic screening. Vision-based gait analysis
promises cheaper, contactless assessment~\citep{clinical_gait_review}, and adult
spinal deformity in particular alters posture and gait in ways relevant to early
orthopedic management~\citep{asd_clinical_review}. Purely appearance-driven models,
however, provide little evidence that predictions rest on the anatomically
relevant regions a clinician would examine---an interpretability gap that this
work directly targets.

\subsection{Attention and feature fusion}
Channel and spatial attention modules such as
squeeze-and-excitation~\citep{hu2018senet} and {CBAM}~\citep{woo2018cbam} re-weight
features to emphasise informative channels or locations, and self-attention /
transformer mechanisms~\citep{vaswani2017attention,dosovitskiy2021vit} model
pairwise interactions across a feature map. In almost all of these, the attention
is learned \emph{end-to-end and unsupervised}: nothing ties it to a clinically
meaningful region. We instead treat a doctor-annotated region of interest as an
additional input stream and combine it with the RGB features through a learned
channel-wise gate, comparing against early fusion, squeeze-and-excitation, and
{QKV} cross-attention as fusion baselines.

\subsection{Priors, pose guidance, and supervised interpretability}
A parallel line of work injects structured priors into medical and video models.
Keypoints from pose estimators~\citep{cao2017openpose,sun2019hrnet} localise
anatomy, and heat-map supervision---popularised for segmentation with
{U-Net}~\citep{ronneberger2015unet} and Dice-based
objectives~\citep{milletari2016vnet}---provides dense spatial targets. Post-hoc
explanation methods such as {CAM} and {Grad-CAM}~\citep{zhou2016cam,selvaraju2017gradcam}
visualise where a trained network attends, and saliency-evaluation
metrics~\citep{judd2009learning,bylinskii2019metrics} quantify how well such maps
match a reference. Rather than explain a model after the fact, we \emph{supervise}
per-joint side-head attention against expert annotations during training and then
\emph{measure} the resulting alignment on held-out patients with the same
saliency metrics, turning interpretability into a quantitative, verifiable claim.
Our work differs from the above in three ways: the attention is supervised by
explicit clinical ROI annotations rather than learned freely; we systematically
study \emph{where} and \emph{how much} of the prior to inject; and we report the
attention--ROI alignment quantitatively on unseen patients.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Overview}
The backbone is a SlowFast-family \emph{SlowR50} 3D
CNN~\citep{feichtenhofer2019slowfast,he2016resnet} (a stem followed by four
residual stages, denoted $0\!-\!4$), initialised from {Kinetics}
pretraining~\citep{kay2017kinetics}. Two inputs are provided per clip: the RGB
gait video $x\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times T\times H\times W}$ and a clinical attention
map $a\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times T\times H\times W}$ obtained from body keypoints (a
confidence-weighted Gaussian heat map over doctor-annotated joints). PoseGated
fuses $a$ into the RGB feature stream at one or more stages. Figure~\ref{fig:methods}
and the ablations below study the two design axes we regard as central:
\emph{where} the prior enters the backbone and \emph{how much} auxiliary
supervision accompanies it.

\subsection{Clinical attention-map construction}
For each frame, body keypoints are extracted with an off-the-shelf pose
estimator~\citep{jocher2023yolov8} (of the wider family
of~\citealp{cao2017openpose,sun2019hrnet}) and the joints that orthopedic
experts associate with the pathology (lumbar, pelvis, head, shoulder) are
rendered as a confidence-weighted {2D} Gaussian heat map, stacked over time to
form $a$. The same annotated joints define the per-joint targets $A_i$ used to
supervise the side heads (Section~\ref{sec:method}, below). The attention map is
a single-channel spatial prior---it marks \emph{where} to look, not a second
appearance stream---and is resized to the backbone's spatial resolution at each
fused stage.

\subsection{Channel-wise gated fusion}
At a fused stage $i$ with feature $f_i$, the attention map is projected to the
feature's channel width and combined through a learned channel-wise gate
$g_i\in[0,1]$:
\begin{equation}
\hat f_i = g_i \odot f_i + (1-g_i)\odot \phi_i(a),
\end{equation}
where $\phi_i$ resizes and projects $a$ and $\odot$ is channel-wise
multiplication. The gate is initialised with a bias $b$ so that early training
favours the RGB branch when $b>0$; we ablate $b\in\{2.0,0.0,-1.0\}$.

\subsection{Side-head clinical supervision}
At each fused stage a lightweight side head predicts a per-joint heat map, which
is supervised against the (resized) doctor annotation $A_i$ with a combined
binary cross-entropy and Dice loss~\citep{milletari2016vnet,ronneberger2015unet}.
The total objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}}
  + \sum_i \lambda_i\, \mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{attn}}_i
  + w_{\mathrm{bg}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bg}}
  + w_{\mathrm{tmp}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tmp}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cls}}$ is the classification cross-entropy,
$\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{attn}}_i$ is the BCE\,+\,Dice attention loss,
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bg}}$ suppresses attention outside the annotated union
(background), and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tmp}}$ is a temporal total-variation
smoothness term. We ablate the presence of the side heads and of the
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{bg}}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tmp}}$ terms.

\subsection{Fusion location and depth}
We denote by \emph{single}-$[i]$ a model that fuses only at stage $i$, and by
\emph{multi}-$[0..i]$ a model that fuses at stages $0$ through $i$. This lets us
separate the effect of \emph{where} a single injection helps most from the effect
of \emph{how many} stages are fused.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Dataset}
The dataset comprises side-view walking video of $81$ subjects spanning three
diagnostic groups---ASD ($54$ subjects), degenerative hip--spine (DHS, $16$),
and lumbar canal stenosis / hip osteoarthritis (LCS-HipOA, $11$)---each labelled
from clinical assessment, yielding $1{,}954$ gait video clips ($1{,}045$
ASD, $585$ DHS, $324$ LCS-HipOA). Each clip is divided into non-overlapping
$16$-frame chunks (any trailing partial chunk discarded), spatially resized to
$224\times224$, giving about $42{,}500$ chunks in total; in the 3-fold split each
test fold contains on the order of $12{,}000\!-\!16{,}000$ chunks. The clinical attention maps are built
from extracted keypoints and expert joint annotations. The dataset is not public
due to ethical constraints.

\subsection{Protocol}
We use patient-grouped stratified 3-fold cross-validation (StratifiedGroupKFold)
so that no patient appears in both training and test folds, with class-balancing
over-sampling on the training split. Each configuration is trained per fold on a
separate GPU. Unless stated otherwise, the ``full'' PoseGated configuration fuses
at all stages ($[0\!-\!4]$) with gate bias $2.0$, side heads, and all loss terms.

\subsection{Implementation details}
All models share the same {Kinetics}-pretrained {SlowR50}
backbone~\citep{feichtenhofer2019slowfast} ($50$ layers, with batch
normalization~\citealp{ioffe2015batchnorm}), with the stem convolution and final
classification layer adapted to the single-channel prior and the three-way task. Inputs are $16$-frame clips at $224\times224$; the RGB
stream is scaled to $[0,1]$ and the attention map is resized with the matching
spatial transform (no aggressive photometric or geometric augmentation, to keep
the clinical prior spatially faithful). We optimise with
{Adam}~\citep{kingma2015adam} (learning rate $1\!\times\!10^{-4}$,
$\beta=(0.5,0.999)$) and a cosine-annealing
schedule~\citep{loshchilov2017sgdr}, batch size $16$, for up to $50$ epochs with
early stopping (patience $10$) on the validation accuracy; the best checkpoint per
fold is used for testing. The multi-task weights are
$\lambda=\{0.25,0.5,0.75,1.0\}$ over the side heads, $w_{\mathrm{bg}}{=}0.2$, and
$w_{\mathrm{tmp}}{=}0.05$. Class imbalance is handled by over-sampling the
training split only (rather than a re-weighted loss such as focal
loss~\citep{lin2017focal}); test folds are left untouched. Implementation uses
{PyTorch}~\citep{paszke2019pytorch} with {PyTorch Lightning} and {Hydra}
configuration; each fold is trained on a single GPU.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol and metrics}
\label{sec:protocol}
We report accuracy at the \emph{gait-clip} level---one prediction per gait-cycle
clip, the unit used by prior work on this cohort~\citep{chen2023two,chen2024phasemix}.
The backbone consumes fixed $16$-frame windows, so each gait clip is split into
non-overlapping $16$-frame chunks at training and inference time; the per-chunk
class probabilities are then averaged \emph{within each gait clip} to give one
clip prediction. We deliberately do \emph{not} report per-chunk accuracy: because
a clip yields many correlated chunks, per-chunk pooling over-counts and inflates
the apparent performance and its significance.

Evaluation uses patient-grouped $3$-fold cross-validation with a \emph{held-out}
protocol: within each fold the outer StratifiedGroupKFold split provides a
patient-disjoint test set, and a second, inner patient-grouped split carves a
validation set out of the training patients for early stopping and checkpoint
selection. Training clips are class-balanced by over-sampling; validation and
test are left natural. Train, validation and test therefore share no patient, and
the checkpoint is never selected on the test set. We report clip-level accuracy
and macro $F_1$ as mean\,$\pm$\,std over the $3$ folds. For interpretability we
compare each side head's predicted heat map with the doctor ROI on the held-out
test patients using standard saliency-evaluation metrics: correlation coefficient
(CC), normalized scanpath saliency (NSS), pointing game (PG), AUC-Judd, and
top-$20\%$ Dice~\citep{bylinskii2019metrics}.

\subsection{Main comparison}
Table~\ref{tab:main} reports the RGB-only baseline, the strongest configuration
of each competing fusion mechanism, and PoseGated. PoseGated's best
configuration (shallow \emph{multi}-$[0,1]$) reaches $94.8\%$, about $4.1$ points

saved predictions are paired across methods within each fold (shared split,

\subsection{Gating mechanism vs.\ plain injection (A6)}
\label{sec:gatemech}
To ask whether the \emph{learned gate} itself matters, or whether the benefit is
simply injecting the prior at the shallow $[0,1]$ stages, we replace the gate at
those same layers---keeping the inputs and side heads identical---by (i) a plain
additive injection ($\hat f = f + \phi(a)$) and (ii) a fixed equal mix
($\hat f = \tfrac12 f + \tfrac12\phi(a)$). The learned gate ($94.8\%$) is matched
almost exactly by plain addition ($94.0\pm2.9\%$) and clearly beats the fixed mix
($91.3\pm3.1\%$). Two things follow: the \emph{location} of the injection is the
dominant factor---all three shallow variants stay well above the deeper and
all-stage models---while the learned gate contributes only a small margin
($\sim\!0.8$ point) over unweighted addition, and a rigid equal mix that cannot
down-weight the prior is the worst of the three. The gate is therefore a useful
but secondary refinement on top of shallow injection, consistent with the primacy
of fusion location throughout.

\subsection{Interpretability: attention vs.\ clinical ROI}
\label{sec:interp}
Table~\ref{tab:align} and Fig.~\ref{fig:align_fig} quantify, on held-out test
patients, how well the supervised side-head attention matches the doctor ROI.
Because the shallow classifier of Section~\ref{sec:layers} (multi-$[0,1]$) carries
side heads only at stages $0\!-\!1$, we study alignment \emph{across depth} on a
deeper PoseGated variant instrumented with side heads through stage~$3$
(multi-$[0,1,2,3]$). The attention peak lands inside the clinical ROI in
essentially every frame (PG $=1.00$), the ranking of ROI pixels is near-perfect
(AUC $\approx 0.998$), and correlation with the annotation is highest at the
deepest side head ($L3$, CC up to $0.76$), consistently across the three
diagnostic classes. Figure~\ref{fig:qual}(b) shows the effect qualitatively:
on a correctly-classified held-out clip per class, the $L3$ supervised attention
concentrates on the annotated joints, echoing the doctor ROI. For contrast,
Figure~\ref{fig:qual}(a) shows where the classifier itself looks---Grad-CAM
\citep{selvaraju2017gradcam} at the shared backbone's last stage: PoseGated's
class-discriminative attention is the most spatially focused and body-centred,
whereas the RGB baseline and the unsupervised SE and cross-attention variants are
more diffuse and often drift toward the legs or background.

\subsection{Is the clinical prior actually used?}
\label{sec:necessity}
A natural concern is whether PoseGated's gain comes from the clinical prior or
merely from the extra parameters of the fusion path. We probe this at test time on
the trained main model (multi-$[0,1]$) by replacing the doctor ROI with (i) a
\emph{shuffled} map---another clip's ROI, which preserves the map statistics but
breaks the video--ROI correspondence---and (ii) a \emph{zero} map (no prior),
on a stratified subsample of each fold's test set (Table~\ref{tab:necessity}).
Removing the prior collapses accuracy from $94.4\%$ to $73.0\%$ ($-21.4$), and
even a wrong-but-plausible ROI drops it to $90.1\%$---essentially the RGB-only
baseline level. The model therefore genuinely \emph{uses} the clinical prior, and
in a patient-specific way; the improvement is not an artefact of added capacity.
(The zero condition is partly out-of-distribution for the gate, so the shuffled
result---in-distribution, still $-4.3$ points---is the cleaner control.)

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discuss}

\paragraph{Less is more for prior injection}
The clearest finding is that the location and amount of fusion dominate the other
design axes, and that a shallow injection is best. Fusing the clinical prior at
every stage over-constrains the deeper, more abstract features and lowers
accuracy below even a single deep injection. Likewise, the auxiliary background
and temporal-smoothness losses and a strongly RGB-biased gate initialisation---all
defaults of the ``full'' configuration---individually hurt. The practical
recommendation is therefore a shallow, lightly-regularised PoseGated
(multi-$[0,1]$), not the fully-instrumented model. That the post-hoc stacked
combination does not beat the simple shallow model further supports the primacy
of the fusion-location choice.

\paragraph{Why shallow fusion may help}
A plausible mechanism is a division of labour between the two streams. Early
stages ($0,1$) encode low-level appearance and coarse body layout, where a
spatial prior over anatomical joints is well matched to the feature semantics and
can steer the receptive field toward the pathological regions cheaply. Deeper
stages ($3,4$) encode abstract, task-specific motion patterns; forcing a
fixed anatomical prior into them competes with representations the classifier has
already learned to weight, effectively adding a regulariser exactly where the
network least needs one. This is consistent with the two observations that (i) a
\emph{single} deep injection ($L3$/$L4$) is strong---one well-placed nudge is
useful---but (ii) \emph{cumulatively} fusing through the deep stages is
harmful---the prior, applied everywhere, over-writes learned features. The gate
supports the same reading: a neutral initialisation ($b{=}0$) lets the network
choose the mixture per channel, whereas a strong RGB bias ($b{=}2$) suppresses the
prior early and never fully recovers. The auxiliary background and
temporal-smoothness losses, which further constrain the attention, likewise trade
a small amount of classification capacity for regularity that this dataset does
not appear to need.

\paragraph{Clinical implications}
For screening use, the shallow model is attractive on two counts beyond accuracy.
It is cheaper---fewer fusion modules and side heads---and its attention is
verifiably tied to the joints clinicians annotate, which matters more for
adoption than a marginal accuracy gain from an opaque module. Because the prior
enters early and lightly, the backbone remains a standard, swappable
{SlowR50}~\citep{feichtenhofer2019slowfast}, so the recipe should transfer to other
gait-based clinical tasks (e.g.\ other spinal or hip conditions) without
re-engineering the network.

\paragraph{Faithful, verifiable attention}
The interpretability analysis shows the model attends to the clinically annotated
regions on unseen patients (PG $=1.00$, AUC $\approx 0.998$, CC up to $0.76$).
This should be read as \emph{fidelity}: because the side heads are directly
supervised against the doctor annotations, the result demonstrates that the
clinical prior is faithfully learned and generalises to held-out patients, rather
than that the model spontaneously discovered the pathological joints. That the
prior is not merely decorative is shown by the perturbation control
(Section~\ref{sec:necessity}): removing the ROI at test time drops accuracy by
$21$ points and a wrong-patient ROI drops it to the RGB baseline level, so the
model actively---and patient-specifically---relies on the clinical prior.

\paragraph{Failure modes and future work}
Three directions follow directly from the analysis. First, the strongest evidence
for the clinical-prior hypothesis would be a subject-level evaluation on a larger,
multi-site cohort with per-patient aggregation and a proper significance test---the
current clip-level numbers can only motivate, not confirm, a clinical benefit
(see Limitations). Second, beyond the test-time perturbation
(Section~\ref{sec:necessity}), the contribution of the \emph{supervision} could be
further isolated by a train-time control (training with shuffled or absent ROI)
and, ideally, a prospective reader study asking whether the overlaid attention
changes clinician trust or triage decisions. Third, the residual
inter-class confusion (Fig.~\ref{fig:confusion}) is concentrated between the
clinically adjacent presentations, suggesting that a richer prior---joint
\emph{trajectories} or phase-aligned gait dynamics rather than a static per-frame
ROI---may separate them further; combining PoseGated with phase-aware temporal
fusion~\citep{chen2024phasemix} is a natural next step. Finally, all results use a
single {SlowR50} backbone; confirming that the shallow-fusion rule holds for video
transformers~\citep{arnab2021vivit,bertasius2021timesformer} would establish how
backbone-agnostic the recommendation is.

\paragraph{Limitations}
The evaluation uses 3-fold cross-validation; with per-fold variance of
$\pm 2\!-\!6$ points and a best-vs-baseline gap of about $4$ points, only the
larger, consistent differences (baseline vs.\ best, full vs.\ shallow) should be
read as meaningful. The paired clip-level tests in Section~\ref{sec:results}
confirm these gaps are significant \emph{at the clip level}, but we make no claim
of subject-level significance: the tests treat correlated clips of one subject as
independent, which over-states confidence, and with only three folds a
fold-level test would be underpowered. Crucially, accuracy is measured at the
gait-clip level: because each subject
contributes many correlated clips, these numbers over-count relative to the
independent subjects per fold and \emph{should not be read as subject- or
patient-level performance}; confirming a clinical benefit over the RGB baseline
would require per-subject evaluation on a substantially larger cohort. The study
also uses a single 3D-CNN backbone and a single, non-public institutional
dataset; the ``best combination'' was assembled post-hoc rather than validated as
an independent configuration; and the interpretability claim is one of supervised
fidelity, not emergent discovery.

%============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

PoseGated injects doctor-annotated attention into a 3D-CNN gait classifier through
channel-wise gating and side-head clinical supervision, improving 3-class ASD
classification from $90.7\%$ (RGB-only) to $94.8\%$ while producing attention that
verifiably localises the clinical ROI on held-out patients. A controlled ablation
shows that a shallow, lightly-supervised injection is optimal and that the common
``fuse-everywhere, add-every-loss'' default is counter-productive---a guideline we
expect to transfer to other clinical-prior fusion problems.
